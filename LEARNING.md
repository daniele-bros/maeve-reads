# Why Chapter 1 is built this way

Maeve is 4. Chapter 1 is the foundation of word reading: hear sounds in words, learn a few letter sounds, and blend them into real words. Later chapters (more letters, then little stories) stay locked until this is solid.

## The research underneath

**Simple View of Reading** (Gough & Tunmer, 1986). Reading comprehension is decoding times language comprehension. At 4, this chapter builds decoding. The talk around the pictures keeps language in the game, but we are not asking her to read sentences yet.

**Scarborough's Reading Rope.** Skilled reading weaves word recognition with language comprehension. Chapter 1 works the word-recognition strands in order: phonological awareness, then a small set of letter-sound links (decoding), then reading those letters in words so they start to stick (orthographic mapping). That is the bottom of the rope, taught on purpose.

**Ehri's phases.** Children move from pre-alphabetic (using cues that are not letters) into partial alphabetic reading, where a few graphemes connect to sounds. Chapter 1 is that move. She is not memorizing a pile of whole words. She is learning that letters map to sounds and that those sounds blend into a word she knows.

**National Reading Panel (2000).** Phonemic awareness and systematic phonics are two of the pieces with the strongest evidence for beginning reading. Phonemic awareness teaches best when it is tied to letters, which is why the ear games come first and the letters follow quickly. Guided oral practice matters too, so she can say a sound or a word when she wants to.

**Systematic synthetic phonics.** Programs such as Jolly Phonics, UFLI Foundations, and Reading Mastery agree on the shape even when the exact order differs: teach a small set of grapheme-phoneme correspondences explicitly, practice them, and blend them into words as soon as you can. We use the Jolly Phonics / SATPIN starter set **s, a, t, p, i, n**, lowercase first, because those six letters build many simple words (sat, pat, tap, pin, tin, nap, sip, pan, ant). Keywords are snake, apple, tiger, pig, igloo, and nose, each with a body action, so the sound has a picture and a movement, not only a letter name.

Letter **sounds** are taught, not letter names. Names ("ess") do not blend. "suh" is a different syllable and gets in the way. Clips in `audio/phonemes/` are a neural voice saying a stretched sound (`sssss`, `aaa`, `nnnn`) or, for stops, a short burst. If a clip is missing, the browser voice says the same kind of prompt. It does not teach "suh."

## How a lesson feels

**I do, we do, you do.** Pip shows the new idea, they try one together, then Maeve does a few on her own. That is explicit instruction with guided practice, not a quiz she was never taught.

**Ear games before print.** Rhymes, first sounds, then oral blending (onset-rime, then every sound: "c … a … t … cat"). No letters on those screens. Blending out loud is the same move she will make with her eyes in a minute.

**Errorless-leaning misses.** A miss replays the sound and removes a choice. A second miss shows the answer and lets her tap it. There is no buzzer and no "wrong." Scaffolded tries do not count as mastery.

**Short sessions.** About five scored tries, then a sticker and a real stopping point. She can play more. She does not have to.

**Mastery, then the next game.** A skill unlocks the next one when about 4 of the last 5 first tries are correct. Earlier sounds return inside later lessons (a review trial, and old letters as the other choices). The grown-up corner can open any game without waiting.

**First words.** After s, a, and t she blends **sat**: tap each letter, swoop to blend, then match a picture. After p, i, and n the word game uses sat, pat, tap, pin, tin, nap, sip, pan, and ant. Pictures confirm meaning after the blend. They are not a guessing strategy.

## Saying it out loud

When she says the sound or the word, she is practicing the link between grapheme and phoneme and the blend itself. That spoken response is part of learning the word, not a test of the microphone. Recognition of a 4-year-old is unreliable, so a miss from the mic is never scored as a reading error. Tapping is always there. A clear spoken answer gets a bigger celebration because she did the full thing: see it, say it.

## What this chapter does not do

It does not teach irregular high-frequency words (the, a, I, said). It does not ask her to guess from the picture before she has blended. Chapters 2 and 3 are where more correspondences, harder blending, and little decodable stories belong.
